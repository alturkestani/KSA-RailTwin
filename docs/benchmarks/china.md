# China: CR high-speed rail (China State Railway Group; National Railway Administration)

Scope and method. Research from open sources on 31 Oct 2026. Figures are in the currency and price basis of each source, as printed. Price-year conversion is **not done in this dossier**: converted values are generated by `rail.benchmarks summarise` into `docs/benchmarks/README.md` (see README.md). Metric rows are in `cities/ksa/benchmarks/metrics-china.csv`; the one overrun candidate is in `cities/ksa/benchmarks/overruns-china.csv`; citation records are in `docs/sources/bench-china.csv`.

Evidence grade in this dossier. No metric row is grade A or B. The numeric evidence is grade C: the World Bank's 2019 report and its 2014 construction-cost note, both read in full on the PDFs, plus press reports that relay the Ministry of Transport bulletin and a Xinhua item. The National Railway Administration bulletin and the China State Railway Group's audited accounts were **not reached** as primary documents. Two small-town studies (Qin 2017; Liu and co-authors 2024) are grade B, read at abstract level only, because the full texts are paywalled.

Operator and infrastructure. The World Bank (2019) names the operator as China Railway Corporation (CRC) and describes the lines as built by joint-venture companies. The 2026 press uses China State Railway Group (国铁集团) for the same group. I did not find a source in the reading list that sets out the relationship between the two names, so the entity is flagged in the Escalations.

Delivery and funding. Lines were built "through special-purpose asset construction and management companies"[^S-bench-cn-025], normally joint ventures of the central and provincial governments. "Many now operate under the access charge model", in which the JV is essentially an infrastructure financing and contract-managing company[^S-bench-cn-087]. The World Bank reports that the network in service at end-2015 was financed by a "combination of the equity contribution from MOR/CRC and local governments", bank loans, the central budget and construction bonds[^S-bench-cn-065]. Local governments also contributed land and resettlement, and in some cases private capital was mobilised (same page)[^S-bench-cn-065]. The World Bank names standardised designs as a key factor in the lower cost[^S-bench-cn-082].

Totals (cost_total). Investment in the network in service at end-2015 is printed as Y 2.5 trillion (US$370 billion)[^S-bench-cn-024]. The price year is unknown (the source gives no price year for this total). The source does not give an overall cost per km for the whole network beyond the averages above, so none is computed here.

Range across sources. The published per-km figures for the same system span the 2014 approval-year estimates (RMB 87 to 129 million/km for the weighted averages[^S-bench-cn-042]) and the 2017-yuan averages (RMB 104 to 139 million/km[^S-bench-cn-001][^S-bench-cn-002][^S-bench-cn-003]). The two sets are not on the same basis. Terrain and tunnels. The World Bank's Table 1 reports the share of route-km on bridges, viaducts and tunnels for six World Bank-supported lines: 69% for the 350 km/h Shijiazhuang–Zhengzhou line[^S-bench-cn-045] and 80% for the 250 km/h Guiyang–Guangzhou line[^S-bench-cn-046]; the other four lines are in the same table. Chinese lines are described as having a high share on viaducts or in tunnels, but no per-km series adjusted for terrain was found.

Comparison with other countries, as reported. The World Bank states that Chinese costs are "at least 40 percent cheaper than in Europe"[^S-bench-cn-066]. It attributes part of the difference to lower labour costs and to standardised designs and procedures[^S-bench-cn-082]. It adds that "the extent to which other countries can copy the practice of China depends on their individual situations"[^S-bench-cn-081]. Cost comparisons across countries remain indicative (see README.md).

Overrun-type evidence on cost is in section 7.

## 3. Ridership vs forecast

No reliable data found for ridership against the forecast at decision (searched: World Bank 2014 and 2019 texts as read; Journal and RePEc abstracts for Qin 2017 and Liu and co-authors 2024; Ministry of Transport and National Railway Administration 2025 press reports; web searches for Wu, Nash and Wang and for Beijing–Shanghai feasibility forecasts, 2026-10-31). The searches returned no paper with an ex-ante forecast and an ex-post outturn for a Chinese HSR line that I could read. The Hong Kong cross-border line was seen only in a search snippet and is not used.

What the sources do report (outturn only):
- HSR passengers of about 1.7 billion a year at the time of the 2019 report, data year 2017[^S-bench-cn-006].
- Average network density of 23 million passengers per line, against 10 million in the first full year (2009)[^S-bench-cn-007][^S-bench-cn-039].
- Ramp-up: traffic in the first full year of operation about 70% of the steady state[^S-bench-cn-068]. This is an observed pattern across lines, not a forecast error.
- National railway passenger trips (all services) of 4.59 billion in 2025, up 6.4% (press, Xinhua)[^S-bench-cn-051]. This is not an HSR-only figure. Two different 2025 national totals are in circulation; see Escalation E3.

Expectations versus outturn (not a forecast ratio). At the planning stage the HSR lines were expected to carry 90% of corridor demand, with the remainder on existing services[^S-bench-cn-036]. In practice the conventional services were not withdrawn, and on some main corridors conventional lines still carry "from one-half to two-thirds" of passengers[^S-bench-cn-067]. Surveys estimated that 18% of HSR passengers would not have travelled without HSR[^S-bench-cn-035].

## 4. Farebox recovery / operating ratio

The source does not report farebox recovery (operating revenue over operating cost) for the HSR network. No reliable data found (searched: the same sources as section 3, plus China State Railway Group 2025 results reported in press, 2026-10-31).

What is reported instead:
- Train operating cost against passenger revenue, 2016 unit values. For 200–250 km/h, revenue of Y 0.28 per pkm[^S-bench-cn-019] against train operating cost of Y 0.19 per pkm[^S-bench-cn-018], a computed train-operations-only ratio of 0.68. For 300–350 km/h, revenue of Y 0.50 per pkm[^S-bench-cn-019] against Y 0.23 per pkm[^S-bench-cn-018], a computed ratio of 0.46. These exclude infrastructure maintenance and debt service. They are recorded as operating_ratio rows, marked computed.
- Line-level coverage at 2016 traffic. Only five 200–250 km/h lines covered operating and maintenance costs; none could pay interest. Nine of 15 300–350 km/h lines could pay interest[^S-bench-cn-069b][^S-bench-cn-069].
- Fares were unchanged from 2007 to 2016 apart from a 5% cut in 2011[^S-bench-cn-072]. The World Bank attributes the disparity in financial viability between the two speed classes mainly to pricing policy[^S-bench-cn-084].
- Network returns. Financial rate of return for the network at end-2015: about 6%[^S-bench-cn-071]. The HSR-only 30-year financial IRR is estimated at 5.5%[^S-bench-cn-070]; 9.7% for the 300–350 km/h network and 0.2% for 200–250 km/h[^S-bench-cn-022][^S-bench-cn-023]. The economic rate of return is 8% in the executive summary[^S-bench-cn-020] and "about 9 percent" in chapter 7[^S-bench-cn-061]; both are recorded (Escalation E1).
- Shortfalls. The World Bank says the cash shortfall of the infrastructure JVs had been funded indirectly, by the regional administration or by CRC, to that date[^S-bench-cn-079].
- Group leverage (press). The China State Railway Group reported an asset-liability ratio of 62.5% at end-2025, down one point, at its 4 Jan 2026 work conference[^S-bench-cn-052]. This is a balance-sheet ratio, not a farebox measure, and the audited accounts were not read.

## 5. Thresholds: population density, city sizes, and the 200–800 km band

Population density. The World Bank gives China's national density as 141 people per km2[^S-bench-cn-064] and the central and eastern provinces at 420 per km2[^S-bench-cn-073]. It presents these as context, not as an HSR threshold. No reliable HSR population-density threshold was found (searched: World Bank 2014 and 2019 texts; Qin 2017; Liu and co-authors 2024 abstracts, 2026-10-31).

City size. The World Bank says China has many cities over 500,000 population at distances between 200 and 500 km that are "well suited for HSR"[^S-bench-cn-074]. This is a qualitative statement; no size threshold was derived in the sources read.

Density thresholds for the network (passengers per year per line):
- Full cost coverage at the 2016 fare: 35 million or more[^S-bench-cn-015].
- Breakeven at opening, 200–250 km/h: 21 million for maintenance alone and 85 million for maintenance plus interest and principal; 300–350 km/h: 9 million and 33 million[^S-bench-cn-016].
- Network economics (model): the EIRR would fall to about 5% at a density of 15 million[^S-bench-cn-021], and to 0% at about 7 million[^S-bench-cn-059]. These results are "specific to the circumstances of the Chinese system"[^S-bench-cn-085].

The 200–800 km band, tested. The band is a hypothesis for KSA and is tested here only against China's corridor evidence.

Evidence that supports a central band in China:
- Car and bus dominate below 150 km; HSR dominates between 150 and 800 km; 350 km/h services remain competitive to about 1,200 km; air dominates beyond that (World Bank, 2019 reading of corridors and surveys)[^S-bench-cn-009][^S-bench-cn-010][^S-bench-cn-011].
- Air competition: HSR cut flights on several routes; flight services on Guangzhou–Wuhan and Guangzhou–Changsha fell after opening[^S-bench-cn-088]. The World Bank reports that HSR takes "a large share of air traffic up to 800 km"[^S-bench-cn-075].
- Mode shares by distance, 2013 Beijing–Guangzhou corridor (HSR share of the rail market): Beijing–Shijiazhuang (281 km) 73%; Beijing–Zhengzhou (693 km) 58%; Beijing–Wuhan (1,229 km) 51%; Beijing–Changsha (1,591 km) 35%; Beijing–Guangzhou (2,298 km) 40%[^S-bench-cn-012]. The World Bank says the HSR share is "about 40 percent" on the long-distance trips of the corridor[^S-bench-cn-089].
- Air: about 10% of HSR passengers came from air, but about 20% of pkm, on longer trips (2015 surveys, five routes)[^S-bench-cn-014].

Evidence that limits the transfer:
- The fare ratio between conventional and HSR on the Beijing–Guangzhou line was "between 1:3 and 1:4"[^S-bench-cn-083]. The share depends on that gap, which reflects the fare structure.
- The World Bank says HSR "typically attracts 10–30 percent" of trips between intermediate centres[^S-bench-cn-013]. The band is therefore strongest for large-city pairs.
- The bands are the World Bank's reading of Chinese corridors, where fares were fixed for a decade[^S-bench-cn-072]. In our reading they are descriptive, not a counterfactual test. The report says its EIRR results are "specific to the circumstances of the Chinese system"[^S-bench-cn-085].

Verdict: China's corridor data are consistent with a central band of roughly 150 to 800 km for HSR against car, bus and air, with the upper limit moving with speed and fare. The band is not tested for KSA: there is no air–rail substitution study for KSA in the sources read. It stays a hypothesis.

## 6. Station spacing, timetable and stopping patterns

- Beijing–Shanghai has 23 stations[^S-bench-cn-026] on a route of 1,318 km[^S-bench-cn-029]. Average spacing, computed as 1,318 km over 22 intervals, is about 59.9 km (metric row; computed from both sources; the source gives no spacing table).
- Stopping pattern: "few services stop at all intermediate stations" and CRC runs a mix of express and stopping services[^S-bench-cn-028]. On Beijing–Shanghai the fastest train stops at 2 intermediate stations[^S-bench-cn-027] and the slowest at 10[^S-bench-cn-076].
- Frequency: most HSR lines have at least hourly service from 7:00 to midnight[^S-bench-cn-078]. The World Bank says this requires an average load of 4 to 6 million passengers per year[^S-bench-cn-077].
- Station location: many HSR stations are outside central areas and need extra connection time[^S-bench-cn-031]. The bus and car alternative remains competitive on short corridors, "especially if the HSR station is a long distance" from the centre[^S-bench-cn-080].
- Punctuality: over 98% of departures on time[^S-bench-cn-037].

## 7. Overruns (cost, schedule) and renegotiations

Cost. One outturn-against-estimate observation was found. For the Shijiazhuang–Wuhan passenger line (841 km), the World Bank's supervision found that "the actual unit cost was about 5 percent below estimates"[^S-bench-cn-048]. This is a saving, not an overrun; the ratio of 0.95 is derived from the printed approximate percentage, not from two printed absolute values. It is the only row in the overruns file. No other per-line outturn-against-estimate figure was found in the sources read. The same World Bank passage says that, during supervision, actual costs of its projects were close to the estimated costs (same passage)[^S-bench-cn-048].

Schedule. No reliable data found on schedule overruns for Chinese HSR lines (searched: World Bank 2014 and 2019; Ministry of Transport and National Railway Administration press, 2026-10-31).

Plan versus outturn, network length (not a cost or ridership forecast). The 2016 revision of the plan aimed for 38,000 km by 2025[^S-bench-cn-030]. The 2025 year-end figure reported in press is 50,000 km[^S-bench-cn-050]. The length exceeded the 2016 target. This is recorded as a plan-versus-outturn fact only.

Audit and governance findings. Press search results report that National Audit Office findings in 2011–2012 concerned misappropriation of demolition and other funds on the Beijing–Shanghai works, and a 2025 press piece cites an unconfirmed loss estimate attributed to an audit body. Neither the audit reports nor the figures were opened, so no figure from them is used (status: unverified).

Renegotiation. No renegotiation of HSR contracts was found in the sources read.

Financial stress as a failure mode. The World Bank reports that the 200–250 km/h lines could not pay interest at 2016 traffic and fares[^S-bench-cn-069b][^S-bench-cn-069], and that the shortfalls were carried by the regional administrations and CRC[^S-bench-cn-079]. It attributes the disparity between the speed classes mainly to pricing policy[^S-bench-cn-084].

Direction of effect, by study. The evidence is mixed, and in parts negative.

| Liu, Xu, Tian, Liu and Dong (2024), Transport Policy 159, pp. 241–253 | HSR-built new towns for stations opened 2009–2019; gradient boosting with SHAP. Searched and not read: a 2017 Urban Studies paper on intra-city access to inter-city transport nodes (Diao and co-authors) and a 2023 night-light study of station areas (from search snippets; title or snippet only, no figures used). Both are listed as leads.

Verdict. Mixed to negative for intermediate and peripheral places in the sources read. Where the station is well connected to the city and the corridor links a megacity to a nearby second-tier city, the evidence is positive, but the strongest positive study (Zheng and Kahn) was not read at primary level. No quantified peer-reviewed study of an intermediate station's effect on its own small town was read in full. The evidence is not conclusive.

## 9. Lessons for KSA (evidence-based, non-advocacy)

- The World Bank attributes Chinese unit costs in part to lower labour costs, standardised designs and procedures, and a scale supply industry[^S-bench-cn-082]. The report cautions that transfer "depends on their individual situations"[^S-bench-cn-081].
- Pricing and viability. The report links the weak finances of the 200–250 km/h lines to fixed, low fares[^S-bench-cn-084][^S-bench-cn-072]. The sources read do not say what fare structure would be needed elsewhere.
- Density sensitivity. Network returns fall sharply with density (about 5% EIRR at 15 million; 0 at about 7 million)[^S-bench-cn-021][^S-bench-cn-059]. The report stresses these results are China-specific[^S-bench-cn-085].
- Conventional rail was not withdrawn in practice, which the source reports as a planning expectation that was not met[^S-bench-cn-036][^S-bench-cn-067].
- Intermediate and peripheral stations. The evidence reports weaker or negative effects for intermediate and peripheral places unless the station is well connected[^S-bench-cn-031][^S-bench-cn-013][^S-bench-cn-053][^S-bench-cn-054].
- Planning process. The report describes a long-term plan revised in 2008 and 2016 and "rarely changed once approved"[^S-bench-cn-086]. It presents this as a feature of the Chinese system, without an outcome comparison.

## Escalations

- **E1, economic rate of return.** The World Bank's executive summary gives 8%[^S-bench-cn-020]; chapter 7 gives "about 9 percent (including regional benefits)"[^S-bench-cn-061]. Both are recorded. Not resolved; the coordinator should choose which the README reports.
- **E2, cost per km basis.** The executive summary's US$17–21 million per km is not reproduced by the report's own 2017-yuan figures at 6.75 (US$15.4–20.6 million)[^S-bench-cn-001][^S-bench-cn-003][^S-bench-cn-004]. The approval-year estimates (RMB 87 and 129 million/km)[^S-bench-cn-042] and the 2017 averages (RMB 114 and 139 million/km)[^S-bench-cn-002][^S-bench-cn-001] are on different bases and vintages. Both are kept as rows; none is chosen.
- **E3, 2025 passenger totals.** Press reports give different national railway totals for 2025. The Xinhua item[^S-bench-cn-051] is used (grade C). A different total in a search snippet was not opened and is not used. The HSR-only 2025 ridership is not found.
- **E4, operator name.** The World Bank (2019) names China Railway Corporation (CRC); the 2026 press names China State Railway Group (国铁集团)[^S-bench-cn-052]. The relationship is not evidenced in the sources read. The coordinator should decide the entity name used in the README.
- **E5, Zheng and Kahn.** The only source for the price and market-potential effects is a secondary summary[^S-bench-cn-055]. A primary read is needed (a later pass) before any figure is used.
- **E6, grade A.** No grade A or B numeric row was obtained for China. The grade A candidates are the NRA and Ministry of Transport bulletins and the China State Railway Group's bond disclosures. Whether to pursue them is a coordinator decision.
